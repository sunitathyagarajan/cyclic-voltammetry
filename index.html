Cyclic Voltammetry Primer

Before your first scan, work through what sets the position of a peak (thermodynamics), what sets its shape (electron-transfer kinetics), and how the two knobs you will turn in lab, concentration and scan rate, change the signal. Every curve on this page is computed live from a diffusion simulation, so the plots respond to the controls the way a real cell would.

01 · Setup

The experiment and what it measures

A potentiostat sweeps the potential of a small working electrode linearly in time, from a starting potential Ei to a switching potential Eλ, then back again, at a constant scan rate v (V s−1). It records the current that flows as molecules near the electrode are oxidized or reduced. Plotting current against potential gives the cyclic voltammogram (CV): a pair of peaks whose positions, sizes and separation report on the thermodynamics and kinetics of the redox couple.

The three-electrode cell

Select WE, RE or CE in the diagram to see what each electrode does.

Cable colours follow a common potentiostat convention (green WE, white RE, red CE); check the labels on your own instrument.

Three conditions make the current interpretable. The solution contains about 0.1 M of inert supporting electrolyte, which carries charge so the analyte does not migrate in the field. The solution is not stirred during the scan, so the analyte reaches the electrode only by diffusion. And dissolved O2 is removed by bubbling N2 or Ar, because O2 is reduced at negative potentials and adds its own wave.

What the current tells you

The potential is what you control; the current is what you measure. Every electron that crosses the boundary between the electrode and the solution comes from, or goes to, a molecule that is oxidized or reduced right at the surface. So the current is a direct measure of how fast the electrode reaction is running:

Current is a reaction ratei = nF dNdtdN/dt is the number of moles of analyte converted per second and n is the number of electrons per molecule. A current of 1 µA is 1.04 × 10−11 mol of electrons per second, about 6.2 × 1012 electrons every second.

A CV is therefore a plot of how fast the analyte is being converted against how hard the electrode is pushing it. Keep three things in mind when you read one:

  • The size of the current is set by supply. Once the potential is well past E°′, the reaction can only go as fast as fresh analyte arrives at the surface. That is why the peak current grows with concentration (section 05), scan rate (section 06), electrode area, and the number of electrons each molecule transfers (section 04).
  • The current is not the amount of product. Product keeps piling up near the electrode as long as any current flows, including after the peak, when the current is falling. The total amount converted is the charge, the area under the current–time curve: Q = ∫i dt.
  • Not all of the current comes from your analyte. The redox (faradaic) current sits on top of a charging (capacitive) current that flows even in blank electrolyte, plus any current from impurities or leftover O2.

Positive and negative current

The sign of the current tells you which way electrons cross the interface, and therefore whether the analyte is being oxidized or reduced at the working electrode. It does not tell you which way the potential is being scanned: as the figure below shows, the current can stay positive after the scan has turned around.

02 · Thermodynamics

Where the peaks appear

Consider a one-step couple, O + ne− ⇌ R. The electrode potential is a measure of the free energy of electrons in the electrode. Making the electrode more positive lowers that energy, so electrons leave R and oxidation becomes favorable. Making it more negative raises it, so electrons enter O and reduction becomes favorable. The link between potential and free energy is

Free energy and potentialΔG° = −nFE°F = 96 485 C mol−1. A positive E° (vs SHE) means O is a good oxidant: its reduction is spontaneous against the hydrogen electrode.

The key idea for CV is that the applied potential fixes the ratio of O to R right at the electrode surface, not in the bulk solution. For a fast (reversible) couple this ratio obeys the Nernst equation at every instant of the scan:

ΔG = −nF(E − E°′) per mole for oxidizing R at the applied potential. Negative means oxidation is downhill.

Standard, formal and half-wave potentials

E° is defined for unit activities. In a real solution you measure the formal potential E°′, which folds in activity coefficients, ionic strength, ion pairing, complexation and pH. That is the number a CV gives you. On the voltammogram you read it as the half-wave potential, the midpoint of the two peaks:

Reading E°′ from a reversible CVE½ = Epa + Epc2 = E°′ + RTnF ln DR½DO½ ≈ E°′The diffusion-coefficient term is usually a few millivolts, so E½ is taken as E°′.

Why the current makes a peak

Current is set by how fast reactant reaches the surface, which is the slope of its concentration profile there: i = nFAD(∂C/∂x)x=0. Two effects compete. As the potential passes E°′, Nernst forces the surface concentration of reactant down, which steepens the gradient and raises the current. At the same time the depleted zone (the diffusion layer, roughly √(Dt) thick) keeps growing into the solution, which flattens the gradient. Rising first, then falling: that is the peak. 03 · Kinetics

Reversible, quasi-reversible and irreversible

Nernst tells you where the surface ratio wants to be. Whether it gets there depends on how fast electrons cross the interface. That rate is described by the standard heterogeneous rate constant k° (cm s−1) and the transfer coefficient α (usually near 0.5) in the Butler–Volmer expressions

Butler–Volmer rate constantskred = k° exp[−αnf(E − E°′)]    kox = k° exp[(1 − α)nf(E − E°′)]f = F/RT = 38.9 V−1 at 25 °C. Moving E away from E°′ speeds one direction exponentially and slows the other.

What decides the shape of the CV is k° compared with the rate of diffusion on the time scale of the scan. Matsuda and Ayabe expressed that comparison as one dimensionless number:

Matsuda–Ayabe parameterΛ = k°(D n f v)½Λ ≥ 15: reversible.  15 > Λ > 10−2(1+α): quasi-reversible.  Λ ≤ 10−2(1+α): irreversible. Because v is in the denominator, the same couple can look reversible at a slow scan and quasi-reversible at a fast one.
  • Reversible (Nernstian). Electron transfer is so fast that the surface stays at Nernstian equilibrium throughout the scan. Diffusion alone limits the current, and the peaks sit symmetrically about E°′.
  • Quasi-reversible. Electron transfer and diffusion are comparably fast. Extra driving force (overpotential) is needed, so the anodic peak moves positive, the cathodic peak moves negative, and the separation grows as the scan speeds up.
  • Irreversible. Electron transfer is so slow that the back reaction is negligible near the forward peak. The forward peak is broad and shifted far past E°′, and the return peak is pushed so far away that it usually falls outside the scan window.

Do not confuse electrochemical reversibility (fast electron transfer, the topic here) with chemical reversibility (the product survives long enough to be converted back). A product that decays by a follow-up chemical reaction shrinks the return peak even when electron transfer is fast. You will meet that case in the quiz.

04 · Two electrons

Two-electron oxidation and reduction

Many molecules can give up or take on two electrons. On a CV this shows up in one of two ways, depending on whether the two electrons move together or one at a time.

  • One step, n = 2. Both electrons transfer in a single wave: O + 2e− ⇌ R. A reversible two-electron wave is sharper than a one-electron wave (ΔEp = 59/2 ≈ 30 mV), and its peak is 23/2 ≈ 2.8 times taller at the same concentration, because of the n3/2 in the Randles–Sevcik equation. Quinone/hydroquinone in buffered water (2e−, 2H+) behaves this way. Ascorbic acid in section 03 is also a two-electron oxidation, but an irreversible one.
  • Two one-electron steps (an EE mechanism). The molecule is oxidized once, then again. If removing the second electron is clearly harder than removing the first, the CV shows two separate waves, and each one looks like an ordinary one-electron couple.
Two one-electron stepsA ⇌ A•+ + e−    (E°′1)
A•+ ⇌ A2+ + e−   (E°′2)Overall A ⇌ A2+ + 2e−. Read each step left to right for the oxidations on the forward (positive-going) scan and right to left for the reductions on the return scan.

Worked example: tetrathiafulvalene (TTF)

TTF is an organic electron donor, best known as a building block of conducting salts. In acetonitrile it is oxidized in two clean, reversible one-electron steps: first to the radical cation TTF•+, then to the dication TTF2+. On the return scan the dication takes the electrons back in the reverse order. The result is a CV with two anodic peaks and two cathodic peaks. Read each step the same way you would read a one-electron couple. Peaks 1 and 4 belong to the TTF•+/TTF couple and peaks 2 and 3 to the TTF2+/TTF•+ couple. Each pair is about 59 mV apart, which tells you each step moves one electron, and the midpoint of each pair gives that step’s formal potential.

Measure the height of peak 2 from the decaying tail of wave 1, not from zero. When the second wave starts, the first oxidation is still running at its diffusion-limited rate, so the second peak sits on top of it. The same applies to peak 4, which sits on the tail of peak 3.

When do two steps merge into one wave?

What decides the shape is the gap between the two formal potentials, ΔE°′ = E°′2 − E°′1. When the second oxidation is much harder (a large positive gap, as for TTF), you see two waves. As the gap shrinks, the waves overlap into one broad wave. When the second electron is actually easier to remove than the first (a negative gap, called potential inversion), the intermediate never builds up and the CV is a single sharp wave that cannot be told apart from a one-step, n = 2 transfer.

05 · Concentration

Peak current measures concentration

For a freely diffusing, reversible couple the peak current is given by the Randles–Sevcik equation. Every term except concentration is fixed by the compound, the electrode and the scan rate, so at constant v the peak current is directly proportional to bulk concentration. That is what makes CV quantitative: you can build a calibration line from standards and read an unknown from it.

Randles–Sevcik, 25 °C (reversible)ip = (2.69 × 105) n3/2 A D½ C v½ip in A, A in cm², D in cm² s⁻¹, C in mol cm⁻³ (1 mM = 1 × 10⁻⁶ mol cm⁻³), v in V s⁻¹. For an irreversible wave the constant becomes 2.99 × 105 n(αn)½.

Notice in the plot that the peak potentials do not move with concentration; only the current scale changes. If your peaks drift apart at high concentration in lab, the usual cause is uncompensated solution resistance (iR drop), not chemistry.

06 · Scan rate

What scan rate does to the signal

Scanning faster gives the diffusion layer less time to grow, so at the peak the concentration gradient is steeper and the current larger. For a freely diffusing species the peak current grows as √v. A plot of ip against v½ that is straight and passes through the origin is your evidence for diffusion control. A species adsorbed on the electrode does not need to diffuse, so its peak current grows linearly with v instead.

Scan rate is also your probe of kinetics, because it sets the time scale in Λ. Peaks of a reversible couple stay put. Peaks of a quasi-reversible couple move apart as v rises. An irreversible peak shifts by a fixed amount per decade of v, which gives you αn.

One practical limit: the background charging current of the electrode double layer, ic = ACdlv, grows linearly with v, faster than the faradaic signal. At very high scan rates it can swamp the peaks. Record a blank in electrolyte alone so you can subtract it.

Adsorbed or diffusing? Let scan rate decide

Some molecules stick to the electrode and form a thin layer on it, with a surface coverage Γ (mol cm−2). An adsorbed molecule does not have to diffuse anywhere: everything that can react is already at the surface. As the scan passes E°′, the whole layer is converted, so the charge under the peak is fixed at Q = nFAΓ no matter how fast you scan. Scan ten times faster and the same charge has to pass in a tenth of the time, so the peak current is ten times larger: ip ∝ v.

A dissolved species is different. Scanning faster only makes the diffusion layer thinner, which steepens the concentration gradient by a smaller factor, so ip ∝ √v. Ten times faster gives only about 3.2 times the current.

In the lab, plot log |ip| against log v. A slope near 1 means the analyte is adsorbed, a slope near 0.5 means it diffuses, and a slope in between means both contribute (common for molecules that partly stick to carbon electrodes). Real adsorbed layers with slower electron transfer also show peaks that move apart at high scan rates.

Before you go into lab Polish the working electrode and rinse it. Degas for 5–10 minutes and keep an inert blanket over the solution. Record a blank in supporting electrolyte. Use the same potential window for every scan. Wait between scans (or stir briefly, then let the solution settle) so each scan starts from fresh bulk solution. Run at least five scan rates so you can test the √v dependence.
